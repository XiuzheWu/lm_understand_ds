Observations: Reading W1 by input index, almost all rows 0–499 have entries of magnitude ≤0.2 with no clear spatial pattern — these are the wall-border and most inner cells for the “agent”, “moving”, “reward”, and “obstacle” channels. A small number of rows have conspicuously large entries (magnitude 2–6), e.g. rows near input 600–650 (which correspond to channel 4, “previous frame”, on the inner cells), and a few scattered rows in the same range. Those large-magnitude rows are dominated by a few of the 32 hidden units (e.g. unit 17 around +5.1, unit 6 around −4.9 at some rows), i.e. the previous-frame channel is the only channel that strongly drives hidden units. The output layer b3 = (−0.873, 0.703, 0.712, 0.595, −0.092): the “no-op” bias is strongly negative, while up/down/left are all positively biased; W3 has many large entries on up and down rows (e.g. row 3 ≈ down 2.06, up 0.18; row 9 ≈ down −3.07; row 19 ≈ down −2.93) and on left/right rows, but no single action is dominant — the network must choose directions, not just “stay/move”.

Reasoning: Because only the previous-frame channel carries large weights, the policy is driven mainly by where the agent and moving objects were on the last step — i.e. by the agent's own position and velocity/occupancy history rather than by static reward-target or obstacle geometry. The reward and obstacle channels contribute only small, diffuse weights, so the game cannot be one where the agent must navigate to fixed rewarding cells (that would make channel 2 large) or thread a fixed maze of obstacles (channel 3 large). The strong, nearly symmetric positive biases on up/down/left plus the weak −0.09 right bias, together with large opposing weights in W3, indicate a policy that must pick among all four directions depending on recent position — consistent with a continuously moving agent that changes direction to keep itself on a moving target or to avoid leaving a track. Games fitting this: Snake/Tron-style where the agent leaves a trail (previous-frame channel records its own past positions, and the reward is for surviving/occupying space), or a chasing game where the target is also “moving” but the dominant signal is the agent's own previous cell. It is not Pong (no vertical-bounce structure), not a fixed-dot maze (obstacle/reward channels are tiny), and not a simple key-to-door (reward channel would dominate). The previous-frame dominance plus four-direction symmetric policy is the signature of a self-avoiding trail game.
